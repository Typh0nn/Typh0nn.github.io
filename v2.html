03

Four problems worth explaining

Problem, what I actually did, and what changed as a result.

Japan Tobacco International

C-SKU: giving every platform one definition of a product

Product identity differed by system. What finance called a product, supply chain and commercial reporting each called something slightly different — so figures could not be joined without a manual mapping layer that broke whenever the portfolio changed.

I introduced C-SKU as the shared product definition and rolled it out across all JTI data platforms, owning global product definition standards, attribute management and KPI-driven validation, and connecting SAP ERP product development to S/4HANA finance.

Result: seamless integration across platforms — and the foundation that made 70+ market reporting possible at all.
JTI · RoMoBu cluster

From 18 spreadsheets to 30+ reports, on a doubled portfolio

Reporting was assembled by hand each cycle, the Nielsen Local source was being retired for Nielsen Discover, and two specialists left mid-project.

I led the migration into Power BI, absorbed the departed scope, handled the source migration and RADRS data alignment, and built a centralised Power BI web application as the single source of truth for GBS reporting.

Result: delivery cycles from 2–3 weeks to under one week, with twice the portfolio.
Coty Inc.

Four markets, four ways of working — reduced to one

The regional Customer Service master data function spanned Iberia, the Canary Islands, Benelux and Italy, each with its own local practice. Operational silos meant no shared view of what the team did and no shared standard for the data it produced.

I mapped and standardised over 300 services, harmonised the workflows into one standard, and built the automated validation that carried the SAP-to-Databricks migration.

Result: 100% data readiness before cutover, on a repeatable check rather than a one-off review.
Independent

Agents are easy. The controls around them are the work.

Getting a demo out of an LLM is not the problem. What happens when a call times out, when a model costs more than the task is worth, when the output is confidently wrong, and when a human needs to approve something before it goes out — that is.

My platform runs an async multi-provider LLM layer behind LangGraph state machines, with per-call cost governance against enforced budgets, an LLM-as-judge quality layer, and human approval gates on anything irreversible.

Cheap local models by default; frontier models only where they earn it — the same instinct as retiring a manual report.
